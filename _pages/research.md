## Job Market Paper

### Do Economic Incentives Increase Attendance at Blood Drives: Experimental Evidence from Sierra Leone

*Job Market Paper.*

We conducted a field experiment in Sierra Leone, West Africa, to examine the effects of incentives on attendance at community-level blood drives. The study evaluates interventions ranging from cash-like incentives to intrinsic incentives—a nudge emphasizing the importance of blood donation—and a neutral informational message about donation intervals.

Initially, communities were randomly assigned to one of four treatment arms: an intrinsic-motivation message, a sit-down full meal, an 8-gigabyte mobile-phone data bundle, or a 20-gigabyte mobile-phone data bundle. Because the World Health Organization strongly discourages cash remuneration, we selected mobile-phone data bundles as a cash-like incentive. However, after observing that data bundles were traded for cash at amounts below their face value, we created new treatment arms in previously untreated communities: a Le 30 (US$1.30) transportation payment, a Le 60 (US$2.60) transportation payment, or an intrinsic-motivation message.

Relative to the two control arms, the meal incentive generated a modest increase in blood-donation attendance, mobile-data incentives produced larger increases, and transportation reimbursements—effectively, cash—produced still larger responses. Informational nudges, either positive (“this blood donation works”) or normative (emphasizing that community blood donations save lives, especially those of mothers and infants), did not affect attendance.

Overall, the field experiment suggests that direct financial compensation may be necessary to increase blood donations to adequate levels in low-income countries such as Sierra Leone.
